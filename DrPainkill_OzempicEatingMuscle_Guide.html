THE STUDY

The number they actually measured

The whole muscle panic traces back to one real dataset: the body composition substudy inside STEP 1. It followed 140 adults on semaglutide 2.4 mg or placebo for 68 weeks and scanned them with DEXA, which separates fat from lean tissue instead of just weighing you. That is 140 people out of the 1961 in the full trial. It is a subset, not the whole trial, but it is the subset that actually looked at what the weight loss was made of.

Total lean body mass fell 9.7% from baseline. That number is real, and nobody should wave it away. It is also the only number the scary version of this story ever quotes.

Here is what sits next to it in the same dataset. Body weight fell 15.0% on semaglutide versus 3.6% on placebo. Fat mass fell 19.3%. Visceral fat, the fat packed in around your organs, fell 27.4%. Read those numbers together and the story flips.

"Lean mass fell. Fat fell twice as hard. That is not wasting, that is what winning looks like on a DEXA scan."

THE SCIENCE

Why lean mass falls and the ratio still wins

Rapid weight loss never burns pure fat. When intake collapses, the body pulls energy from everything it can break down, and lean tissue is on that list because it is metabolically expensive to keep running. Think of a company in a cash crunch: it cuts the dead weight, but it trims the expensive departments too. That happens with any big, fast deficit, drug or no drug.

The STEP UP trial (Hjelmesaeth, EASD 2025) put a range on it. Across GLP 1 receptor agonist trials, an estimated 25 to 40% of the weight lost is lean mass. Lose a lot of weight fast and some of it comes from lean tissue. That is the price of the deficit, not a hidden property of the molecule.

Now do the math nobody does. In STEP 1, lean mass fell 9.7% while total body weight fell 15.0%. Lean tissue shrank slower than the body around it, so its share of you went up: lean proportion rose 3.0 points, fat proportion fell 3.5 points and visceral fat proportion fell 2.0 points. The lean:fat ratio started at 1.34 and climbed 0.23 by week 68, to about 1.57.

The killer detail is who improved most. People who lost 15% or more of their body weight improved the lean:fat ratio by 0.41. People who lost under 15% improved it by 0.03. If the drug were selectively eating muscle, the biggest losers would show the worst body composition. They showed the best.

So where is the real risk? The drug turns appetite down, which is the point, but that makes eating enough protein genuinely hard. Pair that with zero resistance training and the body has a big deficit, little protein coming in and no demand placed on the muscle. That is a behaviour problem the drug creates the conditions for, not a drug effect, and it is the part you control.

"The person who lost the most weight came out with the best body composition. Sit with that before you panic."

THE OTHER SIDE

What the alarm gets right

The strongest counterargument is simple: a better ratio does not hand the tissue back. Lean mass fell 9.7% in absolute terms, and if 25 to 40% of everything you lose is lean, that is a real chunk of tissue gone. A proportion can improve while the absolute amount still shrinks, and both statements are true at once.

It also matters that this is 140 people, not the full 1961 in STEP 1, and that the trials did not test which diet or training plan best protects lean mass during treatment. Anyone selling you an exact protein number or rep scheme as proven for this drug is making it up.

So the honest position is not that there is nothing to worry about. It is that the worry is aimed at the wrong target. The drug did not single out muscle. The deficit costs some lean tissue, and what you eat and how you train decide how much.

"The ratio is good news. The absolute loss is still yours to manage."

THE PROTOCOL

How to keep the muscle you have

None of this touches your prescription. It is the behaviour around the pen: the part the drug makes harder and the part the data says matters.

1
Leave the dose to your prescriber

Do not stop, start or change a prescribed dose because of anything in this guide or a video. The muscle question is solved in your kitchen and your gym, not by fiddling with a prescription drug.

2
Get a protein target from a professional

The trials did not test a protein number, so do not grab a random one off the internet. Ask your prescriber or a registered dietitian for a daily target set for your body and your situation, then write it down.

3
Eat the protein first at every meal

Start every plate with the protein portion before you touch anything else. Appetite is turned down and you may hit full fast, so eating protein first gets the part that protects lean tissue in before fullness shuts the meal down.

4
Lift heavy for you, every week

Put resistance training on the calendar on fixed days and treat it as part of the treatment, not an extra. Lifting places a demand on the muscle, which gives your body a reason to keep it instead of torching it for fuel during the deficit.

5
Load the big muscle groups and keep progressing

Choose lifts that load your legs, back and upper body, and try to add weight or effort over time as you are able. The goal is to keep asking your muscle for the work it did before, so the signal to hold on to it never goes quiet. Get a coach or physio to set the actual program if you are new to this.

6
Judge it by composition, not the scale

The study used DEXA because scale weight cannot tell fat from lean. Ask your doctor whether a body composition scan makes sense for you, and keep a training log so you can see whether your strength is holding while the weight drops.

7
Flag it if you cannot eat enough

If appetite is suppressed so hard that you keep missing your protein target, tell your prescriber. That is a conversation to have with the person managing the drug, not a problem to solve alone by skipping meals or changing the dose yourself.

COMMON MISTAKES

How people actually lose the muscle

The drug sets up the conditions. These are the moves that turn them into real losses.

✗
Quoting only the 9.7%

People repeat the lean mass number and ignore the 19.3% fat loss and the 3.0 point rise in lean share. They end up scared of a result that, read in full, is a body composition improvement.

✗
Panic adjusting the prescription

Some people stop or change their dose on their own after a scary headline. That throws away the fat loss the drug delivers and puts them outside their prescriber's plan, for a problem that lives in diet and training.

✗
Treating low appetite as a free pass to barely eat

Suppressed hunger tempts people to eat whatever little they feel like, which is rarely protein. That stacks a low protein intake on top of a big deficit, exactly the setup where lean tissue goes fastest.

✗
Skipping the gym because the weight is coming off anyway

The scale moving makes lifting feel optional. Without resistance training there is no demand on the muscle, so the body has less reason to defend it while it hunts for energy.

✗
Trusting the scale alone

A falling number tells you nothing about whether it came from fat or lean tissue. People celebrate the scale while their strength quietly slides, and miss the signal until it is a bigger problem.
