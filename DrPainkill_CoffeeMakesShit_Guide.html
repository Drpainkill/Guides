THE STUDY

What the probe actually recorded

Brown, Cann and Read started with a survey, not a probe. They asked 99 healthy volunteers whether coffee sends them to the toilet, and 29% said yes. Of those responders, 63% were women. Then they took 14 of the volunteers and placed a manometry probe in the rectosigmoid, the last stretch of colon before the exit, so they could read pressure waves directly instead of trusting anyone's memory.

In the responders, colon motility rose within four minutes of the first sip and held for at least 30 minutes. In one subject the probe picked it up inside a single minute, at a sensor 12 cm from the anal verge. Decaf raised motility in every responder tested, and the rise was significant (p<0.02). Plain hot water at the same temperature and volume, 45 degrees C and 200 ml, changed nothing in anyone.

Rao and colleagues pushed it further in 1998. They ran six pressure sensors as far up as the middle of the transverse colon in 12 healthy people and gave each person four stimuli in random order. Caffeinated coffee drove colonic activity about as hard as a 1000 calorie meal. That was 60% harder than water and only 23% harder than decaf. The biggest hits landed in the transverse and descending colon, higher up than the rectosigmoid where the 1990 probe sat.

"A cup of black coffee with almost no calories hit the colon about as hard as a 1000 calorie meal."

THE SCIENCE

Your stomach is snitching

Start with the clock. Swallowed coffee has to leave the stomach and travel the full length of the small intestine before it reaches the colon, and that trip takes far longer than four minutes. Going through the bloodstream doesn't work either, because absorption and delivery can't happen that fast. So when a sensor 12 cm from the exit spikes inside one minute, the coffee itself is nowhere near it. Something faster got there first, and that something is a signal.

The signal is the gastrocolic response. Receptors in the stomach and small bowel detect what just arrived, fire, and the colon contracts on command. Food sets off the same reflex, which is why Rao could line coffee up against a 1000 calorie meal and get a similar result. Think of it as a doorbell upstairs wired to an alarm in the basement. Coffee presses the button in your stomach, and the colon starts clearing space for a delivery that is still hours away.

What carries the message is not settled. The 1990 authors name gastrin as the likely messenger. Coffee is a known gastrin releaser, and gastrin increases colonic motor activity. The other candidates are cholecystokinin (CCK) and coffee's exorphins, compounds that bind opiate receptors in the gut wall. Gastrin is the best guess, not a proven fact, so anyone selling it as settled is ahead of the data.

The controls show which parts of the cup matter. Hot water at the same 45 degrees C and 200 ml did nothing, so heat and volume are ruled out. Decaf still produced a significant rise, and caffeinated coffee beat decaf by only 23%. Caffeine adds a slice on top, but most of the trigger is something else in coffee that your stomach reacts to.

The response is also not universal. The six nonresponders in the 1990 study showed no increase in motility after any drink. Responders skewed female, and coffee aggravated symptoms in 17 of 65 irritable bowel syndrome patients (29%), a group that was also mostly women. The authors link that skew to higher rectal sensitivity in women. That is their explanation for the pattern, not a measured cause.

"Coffee presses a button in your stomach, and the alarm goes off in your colon."

THE PROTOCOL

Run your own manometry, minus the probe

You can't change whether your colon is on the mailing list. You can find out whether it is, work out which part of the cup sets you off, and stop getting caught away from a toilet. These steps use the study's own numbers so your test stays as close to theirs as a kitchen allows.

1
Check whether you are a responder

Over several ordinary mornings, note whether your first coffee reliably brings on the urge to go. If it never has, you are probably in the majority who don't respond, and nothing below needs fixing. The 1990 nonresponders showed no motility change after any drink, so a quiet gut is normal.

2
Time the onset

Write down the time of your first sip and the time the urge arrives. In responders the probe showed motility rising within four minutes, and in one person within a single minute. A fast, repeatable onset is the signature of a stomach signal. Your colon is not processing coffee that quickly.

3
Run the hot water control

On a separate morning, drink 200 ml of water at about 45 degrees C (hot but drinkable) at the same time and place, with nothing else in your stomach. In the study this did nothing in anyone. If hot water sets you off too, your trigger is something these studies did not measure, and the coffee explanation doesn't fit you.

4
Run the decaf swap

Use the same cup size at the same time, but make it decaf. If the urge still comes, you have the same result as every responder in the 1990 probe, which means caffeine is not your switch and cutting it will not stop the effect.

5
Test coffee before food

Do every test on an empty stomach and wait until afterward to eat breakfast. Rao found a 1000 calorie meal triggers a colonic response similar to coffee, so eating at the same time hides which one did the work.

6
Drink within reach of a toilet

Take your first sip at home or at work, not on the commute. Motility rose within four minutes and lasted at least 30 minutes, so treat the 30 minutes after the first sip as your window. The reflex starts on schedule whether or not you are near a bathroom.

7
Schedule the cup before you leave

If your response is reliable, drink coffee early enough that the full 30 minute window happens before you walk out the door. This doesn't change your gut. It changes where you are standing when the reflex fires.

8
Log it if you have IBS

Coffee aggravated symptoms in 17 of 65 irritable bowel syndrome patients. If you have IBS, record each cup and each flare for a few weeks. Bring that log to your gastroenterologist instead of guessing, because a written pattern is evidence and a vague feeling is not.

RED FLAGS

When it is not just coffee

These studies describe a normal reflex in healthy volunteers, plus a symptom pattern in IBS patients. A coffee urge is short, predictable and tied to the cup. It starts soon after you drink and settles once you have been.

Pain, blood in the stool, black stools, diarrhea that won't stop, or a recent change in bowel habits that doesn't go away is a different situation. None of these studies explains those symptoms, so see a doctor rather than blaming your coffee.

Don't turn this around and use coffee as a constipation treatment. The research measured healthy colons responding to a cup. It never tested coffee as a treatment, so it gives no support for that.

"A reflex is predictable. Pain, blood and lasting change are not a reflex, so see a doctor."

THE OTHER SIDE

The samples are tiny

The strongest objection is size. The probe data comes from 14 people in 1990 and 12 in 1998. The 29% responder figure is based on survey answers from 99 volunteers, not measurements. Nobody should treat these as large trials, and the exact percentages could shift in a bigger group.

The design is what gives the result its weight. These are direct pressure readings inside the colon, not questionnaires, and they include proper controls: hot water at matched temperature and volume, decaf, and in Rao's study a meal, given in random order. Two separate studies, eight years apart, reached the same conclusion: coffee drives colonic activity and caffeine is not the main cause.

The weakest link is the messenger. Gastrin is the authors' leading candidate, alongside CCK and exorphins, and that question is still open. The reflex is well supported. Exactly which molecule carries the signal is not yet known.

COMMON MISTAKES

Do not blame the caffeine

✗
Switching to decaf to stop it

People drop caffeine expecting the morning urge to disappear. Decaf raised motility significantly in every responder tested, so they lose the caffeine and keep the urge.

✗
Waiting for the cup to cool

Some people assume the heat is what gets things moving and let their coffee go lukewarm. Hot water at 45 degrees C did nothing in any subject, so cooling the cup only gives you worse coffee.

✗
Using coffee to treat constipation

The studies measured healthy colons reacting to a cup, not a treatment for a stuck gut. Relying on coffee that way is guesswork, and it can delay getting a real problem looked at.

✗
Thinking your gut is broken because nothing happens

Only about 29% of people respond, and the nonresponders showed no motility change after any drink. A colon that ignores coffee is in the majority, and worrying about it is wasted energy.

✗
Testing with breakfast in your stomach

People run the decaf or hot water test alongside a full meal. A 1000 calorie meal triggers a similar colonic response, so the result tells you nothing.

✗
Taking the first sip on the commute

Responders' colons start moving within four minutes and keep going for at least 30. Drinking on the train starts that window right when the nearest toilet is farthest away.

This guide is based on published research, not medical advice. For irritable bowel syndrome, persistent changes in bowel habits, pain or bleeding, see a doctor or gastroenterologist.
