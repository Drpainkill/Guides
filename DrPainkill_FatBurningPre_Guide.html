THE CASE

One tub. One transplant.

A 31 year old woman took a thermogenic fat burner sold for workouts at the recommended 5 gram dose, four to five times a week. She had used it on and off for a year with no trouble. The damage came from eight weeks of steady use.

First the fatigue and nausea. By week nine her eyes went yellow. That glow is bilirubin backing up, not dehydration, because the liver filtering it out had started to fail. Fifteen days after she was admitted, she needed a new liver.

The tub listed more than 30 ingredients and not a single amount. The authors could point at several known liver hazards inside it, but nobody can say which one pulled the trigger. That is the whole problem with a blend you cannot read.

THE MECHANISM

Your liver takes the first hit

Everything you swallow leaves the gut and runs straight to the liver before the rest of the body sees a molecule of it. The liver is the first pass filter, and it processes the whole load at once. Give it a normal meal and it coasts. Give it a concentrated extract and the math changes fast.

Here is the empty stomach trap. A bolus of concentrated extract on an empty stomach spikes how much actually reaches the blood, which means far more of it floods the liver cells in one hit (USP). In a susceptible person those cells get injured: ALT pours out into the blood, bilirubin backs up (yellow eyes, dark urine), and at worst the liver stops making clotting proteins. That last stage is liver failure, and the fix is a new liver.

Green tea extract is the documented example, separate from this case. USP found liver injury case reports at EGCG intakes from 140 mg up to about 1,000 mg a day, and taking the extract fasted significantly raises how much EGCG gets in. EFSA 2018 found 800 mg of EGCG a day or more raised liver enzymes in trials and could set no safe dose at all. The drink is fine. The concentrated capsule is a different animal.

The implicated ingredients in the transplant blend were different ones: garcinia cambogia, conjugated linoleic acid, guggul and chromium picolinate, all with their own liver signals. The proprietary blend is what turns this into a gamble. Thirty plus ingredients, no amounts, so neither you nor your doctor can know the dose of any single one you took.

THE SWAP

A dose you can count

This is not a case against pre workout. It is a case against one category: the stacked, amount free thermogenic tub. A plain caffeine pre workout is not what these reports are about.

The dividing line is simple. Can you read the milligram dose of every active ingredient on the label? If yes, you can titrate and you can tell a doctor what you took. If no, you are rolling dice with the only liver you get.

THE PROTOCOL

Read the label first

1
Flip the tub to the ingredient panel

If you see the words proprietary blend with no milligram amount next to each ingredient, put it down. A blend you cannot dose is a dose you cannot control, and that is the exact setup in the case report.

2
Count the ingredients

More than 20 lines, especially with thermogenic or fat burning on the front, is the stacked multi ingredient category that 68.5% of supplement liver injury traces back to. Fewer lines means fewer unknowns going through your first pass filter.

3
Scan for green tea extract or EGCG

This is the documented hazard: EFSA found 800 mg of EGCG a day or more raised liver enzymes and could set no safe dose. Inside a concentrated amount free blend, you have no way to know how close you are to that line.

4
Check the flagged compounds

Look for garcinia cambogia, conjugated linoleic acid, guggul and chromium picolinate. These are the ones the case authors pointed at, and any of them in an amount free blend is roulette with your liver.

5
Never take it on an empty stomach

USP now requires the label do not take on an empty stomach, because a fasted bolus spikes how much reaches your liver at once. Eat first, every single time, and kill the fasted cardio plus fat burner routine entirely.

6
If you just want the stimulant, buy countable caffeine

Choose a plain product with the milligram dose printed on it. A number you can count and titrate beats a 30 ingredient mystery and keeps you out of the category these reports describe.

7
Treat certification as a check, not a safety seal

Third party programs confirm the label matches the tub and screen for banned substances. They do not make any single ingredient safe for your liver, so read on.

RED FLAGS

When to stop and get seen

Susceptibility varies person to person, and USP suggests genetics play a part. That is exactly why no one sees it coming, so the only defense is knowing the warning signs cold. If any of these show up while you are using a fat burner, stop the supplement and get assessed.

Bring the tub with you. Because the blend lists no amounts, the physical container is the only way a doctor can see what you actually took and work from there.

  • Yellow skin or eyes, the bilirubin backing up
  • Dark, piss colored urine even when you are hydrated
  • Pain in the upper right side of your belly
  • Unexplained fatigue and nausea that will not settle
  • Take the tub to the appointment so the doctor can read the ingredients

COMMON MISTAKES

Where people gamble

✗
Blaming dehydration for yellow eyes

People drink more water and wait for the glow to fade. Yellow is bilirubin, not thirst, and in the case report the waiting ran straight into failure.

✗
Trusting that on and off use is safe

She ran it on and off for a year without trouble. Eight weeks of steady use is what broke the liver, so past tolerance proves nothing about the next run.

✗
Dosing it fasted for extra burn

Taking it before food to feel it harder is the precise condition the USP warning was written for. An empty stomach spikes how much hits the liver at once.

✗
Confusing the drink with the extract

Green tea as a beverage is fine and EFSA says infusion catechins are generally safe. A concentrated extract in a capsule is a completely different dose.

✗
Reading the front, not the back

The front shouts thermogenic and fat burning to sell the tub. The risk is on the back, in an ingredient panel with 30 names and no amounts.

✗
Treating certification as a safety guarantee

A certified label means it matches what is in the tub. It does not mean the ingredients inside are safe for your liver.

THE OTHER SIDE

This is genuinely rare

Honesty matters here. Millions of people use pre workouts, and a standard caffeine, citrulline and beta alanine tub is not what these reports are about. One transplant in one woman is a rare event, not a common outcome.

Green tea as a drink is fine. The problem is the concentrated extract and the amount free blend around it. So the takeaway is not throw out your pre workout. It is know which category your tub is in, read the panel, and never dose it on an empty stomach.

"Rare does not mean impossible, and susceptibility is personal, which is exactly why no one sees it coming."

This guide is based on research, not medical advice. If you notice signs of liver injury such as yellow eyes, dark urine or upper right belly pain, stop the supplement and see a doctor or hepatologist.
