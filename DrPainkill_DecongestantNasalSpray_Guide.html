The Warning

What the regulator actually said

On 30 April 2026 the MHRA published a Drug Safety Update on nasal sprays and drops containing xylometazoline hydrochloride and oxymetazoline hydrochloride. The warning covers three things: rebound congestion, rhinitis medicamentosa, and tachyphylaxis, all tied to overuse. The maximum run of consecutive days dropped from 7 to 5.

The trigger wasn't a single dramatic trial. Expert advisory groups of the Commission on Human Medicines reviewed the evidence, including adverse drug reaction reports. The Royal Pharmaceutical Society had already raised the alarm in January 2026 and asked for packaging that makes the duration limit impossible to miss.

The key line in the update is about misdiagnosis. People mistake the rebound for their original congestion, so they spray again, which feeds the rebound, which makes them spray again. Keep that loop going and the MHRA says the nasal lining and structures can undergo longer lasting changes, with severe cases sometimes ending in surgery.

"The spray doesn't trap you. Misreading the rebound as your cold is what traps you."

  • Applies to sprays and drops containing xylometazoline or oxymetazoline
  • Maximum consecutive use cut from 7 days to 5
  • Don't exceed the dose, and seek advice if congestion lasts past 5 days
  • Older packs printed with 7 days can still be sold until stock runs out, so the box may be out of date
  • Most people recover within about 3 months with early recognition and treatment

The Science

How a squirt chokes your nose open

Xylometazoline and oxymetazoline are alpha adrenergic agonists. They impersonate noradrenaline, your body's own vessel squeezing signal, and lock onto alpha receptors in the nasal lining. Alpha 1 receptors sit mainly in the small veins and alpha 2 receptors mainly in the small arteries, so the drug clamps both ends of the plumbing. Less blood flows in, less fluid sits in the tissue, the swollen lining shrinks, and air rushes through within minutes. Think of a kinked garden hose: the water goes nowhere, and the bulge flattens out.

Tachyphylaxis is the first problem. With repeated use the same dose does less, so the relief gets shorter and weaker, and the obvious move is to use more. The second problem is rebound. When the drug wears off, the vessels can swell back harder than before. You feel worse than you did before you started and read it as the cold getting worse, when it may be the drug wearing off.

Why the rebound happens isn't nailed down. These are proposed mechanisms, not proven ones. One: the alpha receptors downregulate after being hammered, so the same signal gets a weaker response. Two: through negative feedback, the nose may cut its own noradrenaline output because the drug is doing that job. Three: the drug may also trigger a beta receptor widening effect that outlasts the alpha squeeze, so when the squeeze fades the widening is still running. Four: hours of clamped blood flow may leave tissue starved enough that it leaks fluid and swells.

Then there's the bottle itself. Some sprays contain the preservative benzalkonium chloride, and Graf's 1997 review reports that it worsened the severity of rhinitis medicamentosa in healthy volunteers. The active drug does the squeezing, and the preservative may make the damage from overuse worse.

"The squeeze is fast. The question is what your vessels do once it lets go."

The Other Side

The evidence is split

Here's the part the video didn't have room for. Hagen and colleagues (Journal of Pharmacy Practice, 2026) reviewed 18 articles covering 13 oxymetazoline studies and 5 xylometazoline studies. They found no evidence of rebound or rhinitis medicamentosa after 7 days of oxymetazoline at up to 400 micrograms a day, or up to 10 days of xylometazoline at 840 micrograms a day. Some well designed studies found no rebound or tolerance even after 4 weeks of oxymetazoline.

That isn't a lone voice. A 2012 critical review by a medical panel found roughly as many studies against rebound as for it. Watanabe and colleagues (Rhinology, 2003) gave normal subjects oxymetazoline three times daily for 4 weeks and saw no rebound and no tachyphylaxis.

On the other side, Graf (1996) produced mucosal swelling on rhinostereometry in 18 healthy people dosed for 30 days. Morris (1997) measured higher nasal resistance after 3 days of oxymetazoline. Vaidyanathan (2010) produced tachyphylaxis and rebound with 200 micrograms three times daily for 17 days. Ferguson (2001) found objective rebound in allergic rhinitis patients after 3 weeks of twice daily use. Most of those positive studies ran far past a few days on the label.

What survives both sides: a few days at the label dose is not where the harm signal lives. The risk sits in long, repeated, over the label use. Even then, it looks reversible. In Vaidyanathan's study, fluticasone reversed the rebound and tachyphylaxis, and Baroody (2011) found a month of oxymetazoline plus an intranasal steroid didn't raise rhinitis medicamentosa above placebo.

"Five days by the label is not the danger zone. Five weeks on autopilot is."

Red Flags

Signs you're hooked, not sick

The pattern to watch is the one the MHRA describes. Each dose does less than the last, the relief window shrinks, and the blockage comes back heavier as soon as the spray wears off. If your nose only feels normal for the stretch after a squirt, you're no longer treating a cold. You're managing the drug.

Count honestly. If you've used a xylometazoline or oxymetazoline spray for more than 5 days in a row and you're still blocked, the MHRA's instruction is to seek advice. Do that before you open another bottle.

Get seen properly if you've been on the spray for weeks or months, if you can't sleep or get through a day without it, or if the blockage persists after you've stopped. These are signs of possible rhinitis medicamentosa, which is treatable. Early recognition is what keeps it in the 3 month recovery bracket and away from the severe cases that need surgery.

The Protocol

Use it right, or get off it

Steps 1 to 4 are for anyone reaching for the spray. Steps 5 to 8 are the way out if you're already past the limit.

1
Read the active ingredient

Check the box for xylometazoline hydrochloride or oxymetazoline hydrochloride. These are the decongestants covered by the MHRA warning and the rebound studies. Steroid sprays such as fluticasone, budesonide and mometasone are a different drug class and are not implicated.

2
Stick to the label dose

Use exactly the amount and frequency printed on the pack, nothing extra when it fades early. The 2026 review found no robust evidence of rebound at recommended short term dosing, and most of the studies that did find it used high doses or long runs.

3
Mark day 1 and stop at day 5

Note the date of your first squirt and treat 5 consecutive days as the hard ceiling, even if an older box still says 7. The MHRA cut the limit to 5 because the harm signal is tied to overuse, so don't add days on top of it.

4
Check for benzalkonium chloride

Look at the inactive ingredients for this preservative and ask the pharmacist whether a version without it is available. Graf's 1997 review found it worsened the severity of rhinitis medicamentosa in healthy volunteers.

5
Past day 5 and still blocked: get advice

Talk to a pharmacist or GP before using any more. Tell them how long you've used it and how often, because rebound can look exactly like your original congestion and you can't reliably tell the two apart yourself.

6
Withdraw gradually, on a plan

The MHRA's recovery route is to taper the decongestant down gradually while using alternatives, rather than relying on willpower alone. Let the pharmacist or GP set the pace, since the right speed depends on how long and how heavily you've been using it.

7
Ask about an intranasal steroid

Ask whether a corticosteroid spray such as fluticasone suits you as the alternative during withdrawal. In Vaidyanathan's 2010 study fluticasone reversed oxymetazoline induced rebound and tachyphylaxis, and Baroody 2011 found a steroid alongside oxymetazoline didn't raise rhinitis medicamentosa above placebo.

8
Expect weeks, not days

The MHRA says people typically recover within about 3 months with early recognition and treatment. If you're still blocked after following the plan, go back to your GP, because persistent or severe cases may need specialist assessment.

Common Mistakes

How people wreck it

✗
Reading the rebound as the cold

People feel the blockage come back harder and assume the infection is getting worse, so they spray again. The MHRA names this misreading as the engine of the overuse cycle.

✗
Topping up when it fades early

Shorter relief is tachyphylaxis, and the instinct is to answer it with extra squirts. That pushes you toward the high dose, long run territory where rebound actually shows up in the studies.

✗
Trusting the 7 on an old box

Older packs can still say 7 days because existing stock is allowed to run out. The current advice is 5, and those extra 2 days are the margin the regulator removed.

✗
Blaming your steroid spray

People bin their fluticasone or mometasone spray, thinking all nasal sprays are the problem. In the studies those steroids reversed or prevented the rebound, so dumping them throws away the tool that helps.

✗
Swapping to tablets as a workaround

This evidence covers sprays and drops only and says nothing either way about pseudoephedrine or phenylephrine. Treating them as a free pass is a guess, so ask a pharmacist before swapping.

✗
Assuming the damage is permanent

Some people decide the nose is ruined and either give up or keep spraying out of resignation. Most recover within about 3 months with early recognition and treatment, and waiting is what pushes cases toward the severe end.

This guide is based on published research and regulatory guidance, not medical advice. If your nasal congestion lasts more than 5 days or you can't stop using a decongestant spray, see a pharmacist or GP about rhinitis medicamentosa.
