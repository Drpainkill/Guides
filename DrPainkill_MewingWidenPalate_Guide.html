The Problem

The seam nobody told you about

The roof of your mouth is not one bone. It is the two halves of your upper jaw, the maxilla, meeting in the middle. The line where they meet runs front to back down the center of your palate and is called the midpalatal suture. Every claim about widening a palate, from a kid's orthodontist to a guy on TikTok pressing his tongue up, comes down to one question: can those two halves be pushed apart at that seam?

In a child the answer is yes, and easily. The seam is soft connective tissue, not bone, so a simple expander fitted to the teeth can ease the two halves apart. That is a routine, well understood treatment, and it works because the joint is still a joint.

The adult version of the claim is where it falls apart. Over time bone grows across that seam, and once it has, you are no longer pushing on a joint. You are pushing on one continuous piece of skull. Nobody has ever tested whether tongue posture can undo that, and the orthodontic societies have said so out loud.

"A child's palate is a hinge. An adult's palate is a weld."

  • The palate is two halves of the maxilla joined by the midpalatal suture
  • In children that suture is soft connective tissue, which is why simple expanders work
  • CBCT scans of 10 to 25 year olds and of East Asian adults 16 to 50 find the seam mostly closed by the late teens or early twenties, with real variation between people
  • No randomized trial, cohort study or standardized imaging series has tested whether mewing changes palate or jaw shape
  • The American Association of Orthodontists and the British Orthodontic Society say there is no peer reviewed evidence that mewing reshapes adult facial bone
  • The entire evidence base for adult mewing is before and after photos

The Science

How a seam turns into bone

Orthodontists do not guess whether your seam is open. In 2013 Angelieri and colleagues published a way to grade it on CBCT, a cone beam CT scan that shows the suture in three dimensions. They sorted it into five stages, A to E. Stages A, B and C mean the seam is still open. Stage D means it has partly fused, and stage E means it has fully fused. The scale exists for a practical reason: it tells the orthodontist which tool will work before anyone straps hardware into a mouth.

The cutoff matters a lot. For patients at stage D or E, a tooth borne expander, the kind that grips the teeth and pushes, stops being reliable. The recommendation switches to bone anchored expansion or surgery. That is the field's own admission that once bone bridges the seam, pressure delivered through the teeth is no longer enough to separate the halves.

When that happens varies from person to person. CBCT studies of 10 to 25 year olds, and of East Asian adults aged 16 to 50, find the seam mostly closed by the late teens or early twenties, for most people. Most, not all. Some people fuse early, some stay open later, and the only way to know where you sit is a scan. Not your age, not a mirror, not a forum.

Now look at the scale gap. MARPE, mini implant assisted rapid palatal expansion, screws an expander directly into the palate bone and drives the two halves apart hard enough to crack a fused seam. Even that fails in some older adults, which is why success rates drop with age. A tongue is a soft muscle resting against the roof of your mouth. If a device bolted into bone sometimes cannot split the seam, the idea that resting a muscle against it will do the job has no mechanism behind it at all.

So what changes when people mew for months and post the results? Head position (chin lifted), a clenched jaw, a tensed neck and often some fat loss. All of that changes how a face photographs. None of it requires a single millimeter of bone to move, and none of it has ever been shown on a scan.

"If a screw drilled into bone sometimes cannot split the seam, a damp muscle is not going to."

The Other Side

Where the mewing crowd is half right

Kids are a different story. In a young child the seam is open and still growing, and tongue position is part of what shapes the palate while it grows. Saying tongue posture matters in a 6 year old is plausible. The claim under attack here is narrower: an adult widening a fused palate by holding their tongue up.

Adults can widen the palate too. It just takes hardware. MARPE and surgically assisted expansion exist precisely because the seam stops responding to gentler methods. The fact that the adult route involves implants in bone, or surgery, is the clearest evidence of what it takes to move that structure.

And the habit itself is fine. Keeping your mouth closed and breathing through your nose costs nothing and nobody serious argues against it. Keep it. Just stop expecting it to hand you a new skull.

"Good habit. Fake promise."

Red Flags

Signs your palate is worth a real look

A narrow palate is not just a look. It shows up in the bite and in breathing. If any of these apply to you or your kid, that is the trigger for an orthodontist visit, not a posture routine.

For kids the urgency is higher, because the seam is open and the window for a simple expander is real. For adults the question becomes whether the seam has fused and whether the breathing or sleep problems justify bone anchored treatment, which only a professional with a scan can answer.

  • Crossbite, where upper teeth sit inside the lower teeth when you bite
  • Crowded upper teeth with no room to line up
  • A high, arched roof of the mouth
  • Habitual mouth breathing
  • Snoring

The Protocol

What to do instead of pressing harder

This is the route that actually involves bone. Follow it in order.

1
Write down your signs

Go through the red flag list and note every one that applies: crossbite, crowded upper teeth, high arched roof, mouth breathing, snoring. Add any breathing or sleep complaints. This list is what turns a vague cosmetic worry into a clinical question an orthodontist can act on.

2
Book a kid in early

If the narrow palate, crossbite, mouth breathing or snoring belongs to a child, see an orthodontist while they are young. The seam is still open connective tissue, so a simple tooth borne expander can do what no adult routine can. Waiting spends the window you are trying to use.

3
Adults, book a consult with the list

If you are an adult with a narrow palate and breathing or sleep problems, book an orthodontist and bring your notes. Lead with the function problems, not the jaw line. That framing gets you assessed for the right reasons.

4
Ask for a CBCT of the suture

Ask whether a CBCT scan of your midpalatal suture makes sense for you, and what stage, A to E, it shows. Fusion timing varies between people, so your age alone cannot answer this. The scan is the only thing that tells you whether your seam is open or bridged with bone.

5
Ask what your stage means

Stages A to C mean open, D and E mean partly or fully fused. Ask directly whether a tooth borne expander can still work for you, or whether bone anchored or surgical expansion is the realistic option. This one answer decides every step after it.

6
Ask whether MARPE fits you

If you are at stage D or E, ask whether MARPE or surgically assisted expansion is appropriate in your case and how likely it is to succeed at your age. Success falls as the seam fuses, so ask for your odds, not a general sales pitch. Treat it as the tool that moves bone, not a promise about your face or your sleep.

7
Keep the closed mouth habit

Lips together, breathe through your nose, as a daily default. It is a fine habit with no downside. File it under habits, not bone treatment.

8
Judge by scans, not selfies

When someone shows you a mewing transformation, check it against the split: head angle, jaw clench, neck tension, fat loss. Ask whether there is imaging of the bone. There never is.

Common Mistakes

How people waste the window

✗
Mistaking a clench for a jaw

People lift their chin, clench their jaw and tense their neck, then read the sharper outline as bone growth. It is posture, and it disappears the moment they relax.

✗
Waiting out a kid's crossbite

Parents hope a child will grow out of it or fix it with tongue posture. Meanwhile the seam keeps maturing, and the easy, open seam treatment slips toward the harder adult options.

✗
Using mewing to treat snoring

Someone with snoring or mouth breathing tries a posture routine instead of seeing a professional. Those are the exact signs that justify a real assessment, and a routine delays it.

✗
Assuming one fusion age fits everyone

People decide they are too old, or still young enough, based on a number they read online. Fusion varies between individuals, and only a CBCT of the suture shows where yours actually is.

✗
Expecting a tooth borne expander to work on a fused seam

At stage D or E, the expander that works in kids stops being reliable. Skipping the scan and the staging question risks treatment built for a seam you no longer have.

✗
Treating photos as evidence

Before and after shots change angle, lighting, head position and body fat all at once. That is the entire evidence base for adult mewing, and it measures none of the bone.

This guide is based on published research and is not medical advice. For a narrow palate, crossbite, mouth breathing, snoring or sleep problems, see an orthodontist or qualified medical professional.
