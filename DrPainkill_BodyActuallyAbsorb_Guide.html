THE STUDY

The 30 gram cap was never real

Start with the word that broke everything: absorption. Your gut absorbs nearly all the protein you eat. It does not stop at 30 grams and flush the rest down the toilet. The real question was always a different one: how much of that protein actually ends up built into muscle.

Trommelen and colleagues in van Loon's lab at Maastricht University went after exactly that question. Healthy young men did a whole body resistance workout, then ate 0, 25 or 100 grams of milk protein. The team ran four stable isotope tracers at the same time. That let them follow the meal's protein into the blood and into muscle, while also tracking how much muscle was being broken down and how many amino acids were being burned for fuel.

The answer is printed in the paper's own title: the anabolic response to protein 'has no upper limit in magnitude and duration'. Bigger dose, bigger response, longer response. The authors' verdict on the old 25 gram view is blunt: the response 'has previously been underestimated'.

"Your gut was never the bottleneck. The stopwatch was."

  • 100 g of milk protein produced a greater anabolic response than 25 g
  • The response from 100 g lasted more than 12 hours
  • More of the meal's amino acids appeared in the blood and were built into muscle protein
  • Muscle protein synthesis rates were higher after 100 g
  • Protein breakdown and amino acid burning (oxidation) barely changed

THE SCIENCE

A drip, not a bucket

The myth runs on one mental picture: your muscle is a bucket that holds 30 grams. Pour in 100 and 70 grams spill over the side and get burned. The picture fails at the first step, because protein does not land in your bloodstream all at once. It gets broken down into amino acids and absorbed over hours.

That makes a big dose a drip, not a pour. A bigger dose does not overflow faster. It runs longer. Your muscle keeps pulling amino acids in and building for as long as the drip lasts, and after the 100 gram meal the drip was still feeding growth more than 12 hours later. More of the meal's amino acids showed up in the blood, more were built into muscle protein, and synthesis rates ran higher.

Now check the 'extra just gets burned' story against the numbers. If the surplus were being torched for fuel, amino acid oxidation should have jumped. It barely moved. Protein breakdown barely moved either, so the gain did not come from the body tearing down less tissue or burning more fuel. It came from building more.

So where did 30 grams come from? Earlier studies measured only a few hours after the workout, and inside that window muscle building leveled off around 20 to 25 grams of fast protein. Stop the clock early and a long drip looks exactly like a short one that hit a ceiling. That is timing a marathon and clicking the stopwatch off at the first mile, then announcing nobody can run past it.

"Stop the clock at hour three and a 12 hour drip looks like a ceiling."

THE OTHER SIDE

Where the nuance bites

The per meal cap is dead. The daily ceiling is not. Morton and colleagues pooled 49 trials and 1,863 people in the British Journal of Sports Medicine in 2018. Extra protein boosted strength and lean mass gains from lifting, but nothing extra showed up above about 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight per day. More protein does not mean more muscle without limit.

Spreading it out still carries a small edge. Areta and colleagues gave 80 grams of whey over 12 hours after training. Four servings of 20 grams every 3 hours beat both two servings of 40 grams and eight servings of 10 grams for muscle protein synthesis. Distribution matters. It just matters less than the bro science made it sound, and it is nowhere near a reason to bin a big dinner.

Then there is who got tested. Trommelen studied young men, after one whole body lifting session, eating 100 grams of milk protein. That proves the bucket myth wrong. It does not prove every meal should be 100 grams for every person.

"One big meal is fine. One big meal is not a plan."

THE PROTOCOL

Your protein plan, exactly

The order matters: daily total first, distribution second, the size of any single meal last. Get the first one right and you have captured most of what protein can do for you.

1
Get your weight in kilograms

Weigh yourself and convert if you think in pounds (divide pounds by 2.2). Every number in this plan scales off this one, so use your real current weight, not a guess.

2
Multiply by 1.6

Body weight in kg times 1.6 is your daily protein target: an 80 kg lifter lands at 128 grams. In pounds it is roughly 0.7 grams per pound. This is where gains plateaued across 49 trials, so it is the number that does the heavy lifting.

3
Pick 3 or 4 meals

Divide your daily target by your meal count: 128 grams becomes 32 grams across four meals or about 43 grams across three. Four is the closer match to the Areta result, but three you actually stick to beats four you skip.

4
Space them out when it is easy

If your day allows it, leave roughly 3 hours between protein feedings. In Areta's study, 20 grams every 3 hours beat both 40 grams twice and 10 grams eight times. This is the small edge, so take it when it costs you nothing.

5
Let the big dinner stand

If your day ends with a large protein meal, eat it and count it toward the total. The drip from a big dose keeps feeding muscle for hours, it does not spill over at 30 grams. Hitting the daily number with one heavy meal beats missing it with neat little portions.

6
Stop sprinting to the shaker

Missed the meal right after training? Not a disaster. After a whole body session, the 100 gram meal kept building muscle for more than 12 hours, which makes the panic over a narrow window look like what it is.

7
Spend on food, not timing

Skip anything sold as an absorption hack or a timing trick. Your gut already absorbs nearly all the protein you eat. The money goes further on actual protein that gets you to 1.6 grams per kilo.

RED FLAGS

Check with your doctor first

If you have kidney disease, do not use the 1.6 calculator. Get your protein target from your doctor, full stop.

Keep the evidence in its lane too. The 100 gram finding comes from healthy young men after one lifting session with milk protein. The daily target comes from Morton's pooled trials of people lifting. If you sit outside those groups, treat this plan as a starting point and talk it through with a professional.

COMMON MISTAKES

How people waste their protein

✗
Chopping meals into 30 gram rations

People cap every plate at 30 grams to dodge imaginary waste, then end the day well under target. They protect a ceiling that does not exist and miss the daily total that actually drives gains.

✗
Thinking more is always more

Some read 'no upper limit' and push intake far past 1.6 grams per kilo. Morton found nothing extra above that point, so the surplus buys food bills, not muscle.

✗
Deciding distribution is dead

The opposite overcorrection is cramming everything into one meal on purpose. Areta showed 4 x 20 g beat 2 x 40 g, so splitting still earns a small edge whenever it is easy.

✗
Turning one study into everyone's rule

Trommelen tested young men after one whole body session with milk protein. Treating it as proof that every meal should be 100 grams for anyone stretches the result past what it showed.

✗
Paying for absorption and timing hacks

Products sold to 'boost absorption' or nail a magic window are solving a problem your gut already handles. That money is better spent on protein that gets you to your daily number.

This guide is based on published research and is not medical advice. If you have kidney disease or any medical condition, get your protein target from your doctor or a registered dietitian.
